#+title: Audio on nixOS
#+author: piotr-yuxuan

nixOS plays sound through PipeWire, and WirePlumber decides which
device each stream goes to. Its three outputs are a Bluetooth speaker,
a pair of LDAC headphones and the laptop's own speakers. This page
explains what the configuration changes in that chain, what it leaves
alone, and why. The owner's actions are in
[[file:../how-to/nixOS-fix-audio-playback.org][Fix audio playback on nixOS]]; the settings are in the
=services.pipewire= block and the Firejail wrappers of
[[file:../../hosts/nixOS.nix][the host configuration]].

Devices are named here by their role, not by their make: the list of
paired devices lives in a file that this repository does not publish.
Node names are written with placeholders, as
=bluez_output.XX_XX_XX_XX_XX_XX.1= for a Bluetooth output and
=alsa_input.usb-...= for a USB microphone.

* The outputs and what they offer

- The Bluetooth speaker offers two codecs, SBC and AAC.
- The LDAC headphones offer SBC, AAC and LDAC.
- The laptop speakers are driven by converters that accept only
  48 kHz.

The microphones are the laptop's, the USB webcam's, and the one of
each Bluetooth device, which only works in call mode.

PipeWire runs its graph at 48 kHz, its default and the only rate the
laptop's converters accept. Music recorded at 44.1 kHz is resampled to
it.

* Codecs left at their defaults

The configuration sets no codec. A diagnosis on the machine, in October
2026, found that the defaults already reach the best that each device
offers:

- On the speaker, PipeWire encodes AAC, the better of its two codecs,
  with fdk-aac in its top variable bitrate mode, 5. The documentation
  of the =bluez5.a2dp.aac.bitratemode= property still gives constant
  bitrate, 0, as its default, yet the speaker was seen in mode 5 with
  the property unset. Setting it could only keep that mode or lower
  it.
- On the headphones, LDAC runs at the =auto= quality, PipeWire's
  default. Its adaptive bitrate moves between 990, 660 and 330 kbit/s
  as the radio link allows, so a weak link lowers the quality for a
  moment instead of cutting the sound. The headphones also offer AAC,
  which PipeWire uses when LDAC is not available, for example when the
  headphones' own app prefers a stable connection to sound quality.

=wpctl inspect= on a Bluetooth output shows its codec in
=api.bluez5.codec=, and its configuration in =api.bluez5.profile=.

* Changes not made to the sound path

Each of these was considered and left out:

- LDAC =hq= :: holds LDAC at 990 kbit/s whatever the link. When the
  link weakens, through a body, a wall or a busy 2.4 GHz band, the
  sound drops out instead of stepping down for a moment. The gain over
  the adaptive rates is small next to the cost of a dropout.
- 96 kHz :: most music is recorded at 44.1 or 48 kHz, and the laptop's
  converters accept only 48 kHz. LDAC at 96 kHz spends part of the same
  bitrate on frequencies above hearing, and so degrades sooner when the
  link weakens.
- A higher resampler quality :: PipeWire's default,
  =resample.quality = 4=, applies to every resampled stream. Higher
  values cost processor time on each of them, for differences well
  below what these outputs let anyone hear.
- Other clock rates :: letting the graph switch to 44.1 kHz for music,
  through =default.clock.allowed-rates=, would only move the
  resampling elsewhere: the laptop's converters accept only 48 kHz,
  and a Bluetooth link keeps the rate its codec was configured with. A
  switch of the graph's rate can also click or interrupt the other
  streams.
- LE Audio :: on this machine the speaker offers only SBC and AAC, and
  LE Audio is the youngest part of the Linux Bluetooth stack. It would
  trade a link that works for a less proven one, and its LC3 codec aims
  at efficiency rather than at fidelity above AAC in its top mode or
  LDAC.
- A global EasyEffects preset :: one equaliser applied to every
  output. The three outputs need different corrections, or none: the
  speaker and the headphones carry their own tuning, which their own
  apps set. EasyEffects would also put one more process, and a virtual
  output, in the path of every stream.

* Call mode by hand

A Bluetooth device has two kinds of configuration. High Fidelity
Playback, the A2DP profile, carries music in stereo and has no
microphone. Headset, the HFP profile, is for calls: mono, at 16 kHz at
best on these devices, with a microphone. WirePlumber can switch a
device to Headset by itself when an application records from it: its
setting =bluetooth.autoswitch-to-headset-profile=, on by default, gives
each Bluetooth device a virtual microphone for that purpose.

On nixOS that default degraded the music. The speaker's virtual
microphone had become the default microphone, so any application that
opened the default microphone, a browser tab or a call, moved the
speaker to Headset. The music turned mono and dull, and the switches
came with loud bursts: Headset keeps a volume of its own, here about
20 dB above the music's.

The host configuration turns the setting off, through
=services.pipewire.wireplumber.extraConfig=, so nothing switches to
Headset unless the owner chooses it. The cost is that a call on the
headphones' microphone takes two choices in GNOME Settings, before and
after the call, which
[[file:../how-to/nixOS-fix-audio-playback.org::*Use the headphones' microphone in a call][the how-to]] gives.

Two related choices stay outside the repository:

- The default microphone is the USB webcam's, chosen in GNOME Settings,
  which WirePlumber remembers. A rule in the repository would have to
  name the webcam's node, and that name carries its serial number.
- =wpctl settings --save= stores a setting in WirePlumber's own state,
  where it overrides the configuration. Saving this setting would undo
  the choice without a trace in the repository, so the how-to says
  never to save it.

* VLC's launchers

VLC runs through Firejail and starts with =--avcodec-hw=none=, because
hardware decoding on this GPU crashes it, as the
[[file:../reference/package-guide.org::*vlc (nixOS only)][package guide]] records. Until this configuration, both applied only
when VLC was started by its command, not from GNOME.

The NixOS Firejail module points an application's desktop file at its
wrapper by replacing, in the file, the path of the executable it was
given. That executable is, for VLC, a small script that adds
=--avcodec-hw=none=, but VLC's own =vlc.desktop= names VLC itself, so
nothing matched, and the module only warns when nothing matches. GNOME
therefore started VLC outside Firejail and with GPU decoding, the
combination that crashed. Four optical disc launchers, hidden from the
application grid, reached VLC the same way through the package that
installs the icons of sandboxed applications.

The configuration now builds a copy of =vlc.desktop= that names the
script, so that the module's replacement points it at the wrapper. The
copy keeps the file's name, which the MIME associations of
=home/nixOS.nix= use. Its build fails if VLC's file stops naming
VLC's path, instead of shipping a launcher that bypasses the sandbox
again. The optical disc launchers are removed: the machine has no
optical drive. A test in =tests/nixOS/firejail-profiles.bats= builds
the system's applications and fails when a launcher of a sandboxed
application names a program outside its Firejail wrapper.

Since every launcher now goes through the sandbox, its profile decides
which files VLC can open. Music and videos live in many folders, so the
profile opens the whole of home and the host's =/tmp= to VLC, as the
Firefox profile does for home. What the sandbox still withholds is
what VLC never needs: the shipped profile's blacklists keep keys,
browser profiles and other sensitive application state out of reach.

* Pausing sandboxed players

When an output goes away, WirePlumber's script =linking/mpris-pause.lua=
pauses the players that were playing on it, through their media
controls (MPRIS), so that their music does not move to the next output.
It finds the player of a stream by process: the owner of the player's
MPRIS name must be the stream's process or one of its parents. Under
Firejail's D-Bus filter, a sandboxed player's name belongs to
=xdg-dbus-proxy=, which runs beside the player, not above it. Firefox
and VLC were therefore never paused: when the speaker switched off,
their music moved to the laptop speakers.

The configuration installs a copy of WirePlumber's script with one
fallback: when no process matches, it compares the stream's program
name, without a leading dot or a trailing =-wrapped=, with the player's
desktop entry. Firefox reports the same name on both sides, and VLC's
=.vlc-wrapped= becomes =vlc=. An empty program name never matches, so
a stream that reports none cannot pause a player whose desktop entry
is empty. mpv, which the Emacs music player ready-player starts, is
built with its MPRIS script so that it is paused the same way: see
[[*mpv for ready-player][mpv for ready-player]].

=packages/wireplumber-mpris-pause.nix= takes the script from
=services.pipewire.wireplumber.package=, the WirePlumber that runs,
and applies =packages/wireplumber-mpris-pause.patch= without fuzz. The
NixOS module puts =services.pipewire.wireplumber.configPackages= on
WirePlumber's =XDG_DATA_DIRS= before the package itself, so the copy
replaces the original. The copy follows WirePlumber's updates, and
when upstream changes the lines that the patch touches, the build of
the system fails: the fallback is then ported or dropped, never
shipped stale. The test suites do not build that part of the system,
so the failure shows at the next =nixos-rebuild=, which keeps the
running generation.

The fallback has limits:

- It matches by application, not by process: when a stream of one
  application loses its output, every player of that application is
  paused, including one that plays elsewhere.
- Players without media controls, such as =ffplay= or a terminal
  player, are not paused. They move to the laptop speakers, at their
  own volume.
- A pause that fails is not tried again.

Alternatives rejected:

- Patching the WirePlumber package :: it would rebuild WirePlumber from
  source, with its documentation toolchain, after most updates of the
  stable channel, to change one Lua file.
- =node.dont-reconnect= on the players' streams :: the player stalls
  rather than pauses, does not relink when the device returns, and no
  longer follows a change of default output.
- Dropping Firejail's D-Bus filter :: the session bus reaches the
  systemd user manager, a known way out of the sandbox.

* mpv for ready-player

The Emacs music player ready-player starts the first of =mpv=, =cvlc=,
=vlc=, =ffplay= and =mplayer= that it finds on =PATH=. With only VLC
installed, each track opened a VLC window that repeated it, as VLC's
own settings loop the playlist, so ready-player never moved to the
next track and could neither pause nor seek. mpv exits at the end of a
file, which starts the next one, and ready-player pauses and seeks it
through its control socket, under =/tmp/ready-player/=.

mpv runs through Firejail with the profile that Firejail ships, like
VLC. That profile cuts mpv off the session bus (=dbus-user none=), so
=config/firejail/mpv.profile= replaces it with a filter that lets mpv
own its MPRIS names and nothing else. Firejail cannot relax
=dbus-user none= once it has read it, so the =ignore= comes before the
shipped profile. mpv is built with its MPRIS script, which publishes
its media controls under =org.mpris.MediaPlayer2.mpv.instance-...=, a
name with a random suffix, so that WirePlumber can pause it as it
pauses VLC. The shipped profile has no private =/tmp=, so ready-player's
socket stays reachable, and it lets mpv open only the usual media
folders, such as =~/Music=, which is where ready-player looks.

mpv has no desktop entry, so GNOME keeps VLC for music files. It is
not in =home.packages= either: that directory comes before the
system's on =PATH=, so an mpv there would shadow the Firejail wrapper,
and ready-player would start it outside the sandbox.

* The laptop speakers

The laptop's converters accept only 48 kHz, PipeWire's default rate,
so they need no setting.

Framework's firmware has a setting, Linux Audio Compatibility, under
Advanced in its Setup Utility. Set to Linux, it applies Framework's own
EQ to the laptop speakers; set to Windows, Linux plays through them
without it. Its value on nixOS had not been read when this page was
written.
[[file:../how-to/nixOS-fix-audio-playback.org::*Apply Framework's speaker EQ in the BIOS][Fix audio playback on nixOS]] gives the steps to read and change it.

The configuration adds no correction of its own, such as a high-pass
filter, a limiter or a measured EQ. Any of them would stack on
Framework's EQ or stand in for it, so it waits until the setting is
known, and would then be tuned by ear.
